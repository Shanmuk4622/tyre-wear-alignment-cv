\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
\PARstart{V}{ision}-based tyre inspection must connect a visible feature to a useful decision. Region segmentation, image classification, and boundary localisation are natural components, but a strong component score does not establish that the complete inspection pipeline improves. A segmenter can agree with a large tread region while displacing its boundary; a crop can remove useful context from a classifier; and a validation-selected checkpoint can give a different impression from a fixed training endpoint. These are practical evaluation questions for a system assembled from pretrained models.

This paper studies three questions: \textit{RQ1}, how do checkpoint policy and held-out group affect mileage-proxy recognition? \textit{RQ2}, does predicted localisation improve a frozen full-image classifier through cropping or fixed probability fusion? \textit{RQ3}, how does direct point learning compare with boundaries extracted from dense masks on matched images? The targets are explicitly separate: ordered mileage categories, manual image regions, and horizontal tread-boundary coordinates. None is substituted for measured tread depth or wheel alignment.

We present a task-linked evaluation approach for vision-based tyre inspection that connects recognition decisions to the spatial evidence used to support them. Its methodological contribution is to evaluate each component change against an explicit reference while preserving the checkpoint, held-out group, decision rule, and prediction coverage. This connects three levels of evidence that are often reported as separate component scores: region agreement, downstream classification change, and boundary-coordinate accuracy. The resulting analysis identifies where an improvement transfers across stages and where it does not.

The contributions are threefold. First, an endpoint-aware recognition analysis combines group-specific macro-F1 and ordinal errors to reveal performance changes concealed by pooled checkpoint summaries. Second, paired crop and probability-fusion interventions quantify the downstream effect of predicted regions on a fixed classifier, alongside area and boundary agreement. Third, a matched point-versus-mask boundary study couples coordinate error with extraction coverage and image-level variation, then preserves rejected geometry during video inspection. Together, these contributions provide a practical basis for selecting and inspecting model components under limited-data conditions.

The study combines 153 retained classification runs, 81 localisation runs, and a three-seed matched boundary experiment. Retrospective analyses reconstruct checkpoint endpoints from saved histories, verify group membership, assess explanation-gate sensitivity, and aggregate boundary differences by image and point location. These diagnostics use the existing experimental cohort. Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} shows how the components and evidence are connected.

\section{Related Work and Research Position}
\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Tyre Inspection: Measurement, Recognition, and Decisions}
Optical tread inspection predates current deep networks. Nevin and Daoud evaluate laser/camera drive-over measurements against mechanical gauges \cite{laser2014}; Wang et al. use calibrated laser-plane geometry to recover tread depth \cite{depth2019}. TireEye instead uses visible groove geometry and tread-wear indicators as references \cite{tireeye}. RGB-depth point clouds have also been used to measure rubber tread dimensions \cite{depth2024}. These methods have physical scales or reference geometry that a monocular mileage label does not provide.

Recent learning-based studies address several different inspection tasks. Petrovi\'c et al. combine Mask R-CNN tread-area segmentation with HOG-based condition classification \cite{tread2025}. Their segmentation-to-classification pipeline makes the downstream value of a predicted region a directly relevant question. Surface-damage inspection also targets local defects rather than mileage categories \cite{damage2024}. Mignot et al. aggregate detected defects using attention-based multiple-instance learning to support a tyre-level repair decision \cite{mil2025}; this illustrates why detected regions and the final decision should be evaluated separately. Vivekanandan and Rajeswari combine visual wear classification with driving-behaviour information and domain adaptation \cite{edge2026}. We do not equate their multimodal condition target with the present mileage-proxy labels or compare headline accuracies across these different datasets.

Wheel alignment is an established measurement problem. Lamy and Basset demonstrate camera-based camber measurement with a physical vehicle prototype \cite{camber2010}. Shi et al. combine rim segmentation, three-dimensional point clouds, and registration to a reference condition for toe/camber estimation \cite{alignment2026}. Their use of a geometric reference distinguishes angle measurement from a line or ellipse fitted to an uncalibrated photograph. The alignment software in our artifact is therefore implementation context, not the article's validated contribution.

The research opportunity motivating our work is the connection between a component's spatial score and its effect on the next decision. Tread-region recognition~\cite{tread2025}, tyre-level defect aggregation~\cite{mil2025}, and reference-based alignment~\cite{alignment2026} establish relevant application pathways. \subsection{Evaluation and Representation Choices}
Shortcut learning motivates testing whether a predictor uses features that transfer beyond its acquisition setting \cite{shortcuts}. Leakage analysis and structured-data validation motivate explicit grouping and separation of model selection from evaluation \cite{leakage,groups}. Our group-allocation audit concerns membership, while checkpoint analysis concerns reuse of validation outcomes; resolving one does not resolve the other.

Residual networks and MobileNetV4 provide contrasting recognition configurations \cite{resnet,mobilenet}. CORAL supplies an ordinal threshold formulation \cite{coral}, making class-index error and quadratic weighted kappa useful companions to macro-F1. Grad-CAM and parameter-randomisation checks motivate explanation diagnostics \cite{gradcam,sanity}, but neither supplies a universal threshold for the project-specific gate. We therefore evaluate threshold sensitivity explicitly.

U-Net, DeepLabV3+, SegFormer, YOLO26, and RT-DETRv2 supply region predictions through differing objectives and implementations \cite{unet,deeplab,segformer,yolo,rtdetr}. Boundary-sensitive segmentation evaluation already recognises that area overlap can conceal contour error \cite{boundaryiou}. Our use of two-pixel boundary F1 is related in motivation but is not the Boundary IoU metric. We test whether this distinction matters for the downstream tyre task.

HRNet preserves high-resolution features \cite{hrnet}. Differentiable coordinate expectation and coordinate classification are also established localisation ideas \cite{dsnt,simcc}. Our fixed-row horizontal decoder adapts these principles to the task-specific representation of tread boundaries. The experiment compares a point-supervised HRNet pipeline with a mask-supervised SegFormer pipeline under the same image split and training budget. Confidence calibration and conformal diagnostics \cite{calibration,conformal} are retained in Appendix~\ref{app:confidence}, outside the central contribution.
